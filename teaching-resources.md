Yes. I went through the available material and I’d structure this as a **10-module, roughly 15-hour teacher-development programme** rather than simply giving the teachers 25 random videos.

I’ve deliberately weighted it toward **play-based learning, child development, teacher-child interaction, observation, behaviour, emotional development and inclusion**—which is much more relevant to your preschool than generic “how to teach ABCs” videos.

I’ve also prioritised **Harvard Center on the Developing Child, NAEYC and Bank Street** where possible. NAEYC defines developmentally appropriate practice around a strengths-based, play-based approach to joyful, engaged learning, so it fits very well with the direction you're taking the school. ([NAEYC][1])

## The programme

### Module 1 — Understanding the young child

**Goal:** Teachers understand *why* preschool is different from primary school.

**1. InBrief: The Science of Early Childhood Development** — Harvard
Short introduction to the science behind development from birth to five.
[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/inbrief-the-science-of-early-childhood-development/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) ([Harvard Child Development Center][2])

**2. Experiences Build Brain Architecture** — Harvard
Excellent explanation of how early experiences shape the developing brain.
[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/experiences-build-brain-architecture/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) ([Harvard Child Development Center][3])

**3. Toxic Stress Derails Healthy Development** — Harvard
Particularly useful for understanding why a frightened, threatened or chronically stressed child doesn't simply "behave badly."
[Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rVwFkcOZHJw&utm_source=chatgpt.com) ([YouTube][4])

**Discussion:**

> "If a child's behaviour is communication, what might the child be communicating?"

# Module 2 — Relationships come before teaching

### 4. Serve & Return Interaction Shapes Brain Circuitry — Harvard

This is one I'd **definitely make compulsory**. It demonstrates the importance of responsive back-and-forth interaction between adult and child. ([Harvard Child Development Center][5])

[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/serve-return-interaction-shapes-brain-circuitry/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 5. How-to: 5 Steps for Brain-Building Serve and Return — Harvard

Much more practical—the five steps give teachers something they can actually implement in class. ([Harvard Child Development Center][6])

[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/how-to-5-steps-for-brain-building-serve-and-return/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 6. InBrief: Executive Function: Skills for Life and Learning — Harvard

Explains working memory, self-control, attention and mental flexibility—the skills children are developing when teachers think they're simply "not listening." ([Harvard Child Development Center][7])

[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/inbrief-executive-function-skills-for-life-and-learning/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Activity:**
Have teachers identify five moments during their normal school day where they could replace an instruction with a genuine back-and-forth interaction.

# Module 3 — PLAY is the curriculum

This should probably be one of the **most important modules for your school**.

### 7. The Power of Play — NAEYC

A very good discussion of why play is not simply "free time" and why excessive teacher-directed academic instruction can undermine playful learning. ([YouTube][8])

[Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pC3863lozbk&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 8. Play in Early Childhood: The Role of Play in Any Setting — Harvard

Connects play with responsive relationships, resilience, brain development and core life skills. ([Harvard Child Development Center][9])

[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/play-in-early-childhood-the-role-of-play-in-any-setting/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 9. The Power of Playful Learning — Harvard Graduate School of Education

Longer than the others, but an excellent staff-meeting video. It specifically examines how schools can bring **play and learning together**, rather than treating them as opposites. ([YouTube][10])

[Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kT_CAqc6GLI&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 10. Developmentally Appropriate Practice in Action — NAEYC

A concrete look at what DAP looks like in practice. ([YouTube][11])

[Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NWD3tnW5oAs&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Discussion:**
Give teachers examples of activities they currently do and ask:

* Is this child-led?
* Is it teacher-led?
* Is it guided play?
* What could the teacher change to give the child more agency?

This distinction is extremely important for the kind of preschool you're building.

# Module 4 — The teacher as observer

### 11. Learning to Teach: Observing and Reflecting — Bank Street

This is actually a **three-part professional-development video series**, and I would use all three.

Bank Street describes it as classroom footage combined with reflection on children, materials, environment and teacher behaviour. ([Bank Street College of Education][12])

**Part 1 — Classroom Routines and Transitions**
[Watch Part 1](https://educate.bankstreet.edu/faculty-staff/3/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Part 2 — Dramatic Play**
[Watch Part 2 / series page](https://www.bankstreet.edu/research-publications-policy/snapshots-of-practice/learning-to-teach-observing-and-reflecting/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Part 3 — Early Childhood Materials and Environment**
[Watch Part 3 / series page](https://www.bankstreet.edu/research-publications-policy/snapshots-of-practice/learning-to-teach-observing-and-reflecting/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

These are particularly valuable because teachers get to **watch actual classroom behaviour and analyse it**, rather than just listen to someone lecture. ([Educate Bank Street][13])

**Assignment:**
Each teacher chooses one child and spends 10 minutes observing without correcting, teaching or intervening unless necessary.

Then write:

> "What did I actually see?"

—not—

> "What do I think the child was doing?"

That's a surprisingly important distinction.

[Watch the video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cqO7YoMsccU&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 18. InBrief: The Science of Resilience — Harvard

Explains the balance between adversity and protective experiences. ([Harvard Child Development Center][18])

[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/inbrief-the-science-of-resilience-2/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 19. InBrief: How Resilience Is Built — Harvard

This one is particularly relevant to teachers because it focuses on the role of **supportive relationships with teachers, caregivers and other adults**. ([Harvard Child Development Center][19])

[Watch the video](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/videos/inbrief-how-resilience-is-built/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Discussion:**

Ask:

> "What does a child experience when an adult becomes angry with them?"

Then:

> "What does the child experience when the adult remains calm and helps them solve the problem?"

This should connect directly with your school's expectations around shouting, threatening and punishment.

# How I'd actually run this at your preschool

I **wouldn't tell the teachers: "Here are 26 videos. Watch them."**

That will become homework and eventually nobody will watch them.

Instead, I'd make it a **weekly 60–90 minute professional-development session**.

### Week 1 — "Who are these little people?"

Videos 1–3

**Takeaway:** Children aren't small adults. Their brains, emotional regulation and behaviour are developing.

### Week 2 — "Every interaction matters"

Videos 4–6

**Takeaway:** The teacher-child relationship is itself part of the curriculum.

### Week 3 — "What does play actually teach?"

Videos 7–10

**Takeaway:** Play isn't the reward after learning. **Play is one of the primary ways young children learn.**

### Week 4 — "Stop and observe"

Videos 11–14

**Takeaway:** Observe before intervening.

### Week 5 — "What is developmentally appropriate?"

Videos 15–16

**Takeaway:** Don't expect a 3-year-old to behave like a 5-year-old—or a 5-year-old like a 7-year-old.

### Week 6 — "Behaviour and emotional regulation"

Videos 17–20

**Takeaway:** Connection and co-regulation before correction.

### Week 7 — "Managing a classroom without fear"

Video 21 + discussion

**Takeaway:** Replace threats, shouting and punishment with predictable routines, choices, boundaries and consequences.

### Week 8 — "Every child is different"

Videos 23–24

**Takeaway:** Inclusion means adapting the environment and adult response—not forcing every child into the same mould.

### Week 9 — "Designing the classroom"

Video 25 + classroom walk-through.

Teachers walk around the school and ask:

* What invites independent play?
* What encourages cooperation?
* What creates unnecessary conflict?
* What is too teacher-directed?
* What materials are open-ended?
* Where can children make choices?

### Week 10 — "Our preschool: what do we want to be?"

Re-watch #7 and have a staff discussion.

I'd ask each teacher to answer:

**"What is one thing I will stop doing, one thing I will start doing, and one thing I will continue doing?"**

## One important point for your particular school

I would make **four principles non-negotiable**, and I'd make them part of the training rather than merely putting them in a policy:

**1. No hitting.**
**2. No threatening children.**
**3. No humiliating or shaming children.**
**4. No shouting at children as a behaviour-management strategy.**

Then teach the teachers **what to do instead**.

That's important because simply saying *"Don't shout"* doesn't give a teacher an alternative when 15 preschoolers are running around.

The training should teach them things like:

**Connect → regulate → set boundary → give choice → follow through.**

That, combined with the play-based material above, would give you a much stronger foundation for the teaching culture you're trying to establish.

Also, I'd keep the **children's screen exposure completely separate from this**. These are videos **for your teachers**, not material I'd recommend showing children. The purpose is professional development.

The strongest part of this collection is that it isn't based on one educational philosophy. Harvard gives you the **developmental science**, NAEYC gives you the **professional early-childhood framework**, and Bank Street gives you **actual classroom observation and reflective practice**. That makes it considerably more useful than simply assembling a playlist of preschool-teaching YouTubers. ([Bank Street College of Education][12])

If you want to implement this seriously, the next useful step would be for me to turn this into a **teacher-training workbook for your staff**, with a one-page sheet for each session, learning objectives, video links, discussion questions, activities, and a short quiz/reflection after each module.
